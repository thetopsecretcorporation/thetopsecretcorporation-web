## Brief
Your value proposition is not final copy. It is a testable positioning hypothesis built from customer evidence.

**Positioning** is the context that tells customers what the product is, who it is for, what it competes with, and why it is valuable. April Dunford defines it this way: "Positioning defines how your product is a leader at delivering something that a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about."

The Positioning Evidence Sheet needs five clear parts:

- **Customer:** the specific ICP most likely to have the painful problem and buy.
- **Job/problem:** the progress that customer is trying to make.
- **Alternative:** what they use today instead: spreadsheets, internal tools, people, agencies, incumbent software, or doing nothing.
- **Differentiated value:** why your approach is meaningfully better for this job.

- **Proof:** the evidence that makes the claim believable.

Polished copy cannot save weak positioning. If the customer, job, alternative, frame, or proof is wrong, better writing only makes the wrong idea sound cleaner. Michael Seibel's Y Combinator advice is the right standard: "You want to explain what you do in the simplest language possible."

Clay Christensen's jobs-to-be-done framing keeps the promise tied to customer behavior, not demographics: "The fact that you're 18 to 35 years old with a college degree does not cause you to buy a product." The causal force is the job the customer is trying to get done.

## Exercise
Work toward one shared artifact: a **Positioning Evidence Sheet**. Every claim should be labeled as **observed**, **quoted**, **inferred**, or **assumed**.

### 01. Pull forward the core evidence.
Bring in the ICP, painful job, interview patterns, and current alternatives from the earlier lessons. Mark each input by evidence quality:
- **Observed:** seen in behavior, workflow review, usage data, or current process evidence.
- **Quoted:** stated directly by a customer or prospect.
- **Inferred:** a reasonable interpretation from multiple signals.
- **Assumed:** a founder belief that still needs validation.

### 02. Write the customer job in progress language.
Use this structure:

> When [situation], [customer] wants to [make progress], but [constraint/pain] gets in the way.

Weak: "They need a better dashboard."

Stronger: "When the VP of Operations prepares the Monday forecast, she wants to spot fulfillment risks before customers complain, but the data is spread across three systems and takes four hours to reconcile."

### 03. Extract customer language.
Pull 5-10 phrases from interviews that describe the trigger, workaround, painful moment, cost, desired outcome, urgency, or believability gap. Keep the customer's words. If customers say "month-end fire drill," do not translate it into "financial workflow optimization" unless buyers actually use that language.

### 04. Map value with the Value Proposition Canvas.
Strategyzer describes the customer side of the canvas as identifying "your customer's major Jobs-to-be-done, the pains they face when trying to accomplish their Jobs-to-be-done and the gains they perceive by getting their jobs done." Use that as a translation tool:
- Jobs: what the customer is trying to accomplish.
- Pains: cost, risk, delay, errors, frustration, anxiety, or switching friction.
- Gains: desired outcomes, confidence, speed, control, revenue, savings, or reduced effort.
- Pain relievers: how your approach reduces specific pains.

- Gain creators: how your approach creates specific gains.

### 05. Name the real competitive alternative.
Ask: **If this product disappeared, what would the customer use tomorrow?**

Include direct competitors, but do not stop there. The true alternative may be a spreadsheet, internal tool, employee time, an agency, an incumbent platform, a budget tradeoff, or doing nothing.

### 06. Translate attributes into value and proof.
Use this pattern:

> Because we [capability], [ICP] can [outcome], which matters because [impact]. We believe this because [evidence].

Vague claims like "AI-powered," "all-in-one," "seamless," "modern," or "better user experience" must become concrete customer value.

Strong value point:

> Compared with exporting three reports into a spreadsheet, the product flags fulfillment exceptions automatically, so operations leaders can review risk in 10 minutes before the Monday forecast meeting. Evidence: quoted by four interviewees as a recurring workflow; timing still assumed.

### 07. Choose the category or familiar frame.
A **category** or **frame** is the familiar mental box customers use to understand the product and compare it to alternatives. Pick the frame that reduces comprehension work without creating false expectations.

Ask:
- What familiar frame helps the customer understand this fastest?
- What alternative does that frame imply?
- What wrong assumptions might it create?
- Does it make the product sound bigger, smaller, riskier, or more familiar than it should?

### 08. Draft the positioning hypothesis.
Use this template:

> For [specific ICP] who [painful job/problem], [product] is a [category/approach] that helps them [measurable or observable outcome]. Unlike [current alternative], it [differentiated capability/value], because [reason to believe/proof].

Weak:

> We are an AI-powered operations platform for ecommerce brands.

Stronger:

> For ecommerce operations leaders preparing weekly fulfillment forecasts, FulfillSight flags late-order risk before customers complain. Unlike spreadsheet reconciliation across Shopify, NetSuite, and third-party logistics provider (3PL) exports, it connects the systems teams already reconcile manually and shows source records.

### 09. Test the promise in 5-10 customer conversations.
Do not pitch for approval. Test clarity, relevance, believability, and pull:
- What do you think this means?
- Who do you think this is for?
- What would you compare this to?
- Which part feels most valuable?

- Which part feels least believable?
- What would need to be true for this to be worth trying?
- What words would you use to describe this internally?
- Who else would need to care?

## Deliverable
Produce a **Positioning Evidence Sheet** that the whole founding team and key employees can see, challenge, and use as the north-star artifact for sales, landing-page copy, product scope, and customer clarity tests.

Include:

- **One-sentence positioning hypothesis:** the sharpest current version of the promise.
- **Specific ICP and situation:** who this is for and when the problem becomes urgent.
- **Job/problem in customer language:** the progress the customer is trying to make.
- **Customer-language evidence:** 5-10 quotes or phrases from interviews.

- **Current alternative to beat:** the real status quo, not just direct competitors.
- **Value map summary:** jobs, pains, gains, pain relievers, and gain creators.
- **Differentiated value points:** 3-5 points, each with customer pain, alternative, differentiated capability, customer outcome, proof, and evidence label.
- **Category/frame decision:** chosen frame, why it helps, and wrong assumptions it may create.

- **Clarity-test plan:** 5-10 target customers, test questions, expected alternative, pull signals, and assumptions still to validate.

## Exit Criteria
Move forward only when the checklist below is complete.

- The statement names a specific ICP.
- The job/problem is written in customer language.
- The current alternative is explicit.
- The category or frame is familiar enough to reduce comprehension work.

- The frame does not create obvious false expectations.
- Each differentiated value point is tied to a real pain or gain.
- Each major claim has proof or an evidence label.
- Vague claims are translated into concrete customer value.

- Customers can repeat the promise back accurately.
- Customers compare the product to the expected alternative or reveal a better one.
- Customers identify which part is most valuable and least believable.
- At least one assumption is marked for follow-up testing.

- The Positioning Evidence Sheet is stored somewhere visible to the founding team and key employees.
