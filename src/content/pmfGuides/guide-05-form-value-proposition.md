## Field Brief
Turn what you learned about the customer, problem, interviews, and alternatives into a testable positioning hypothesis. Do not write the final tagline yet.

The hypothesis should make five things clear:
- who the product is for
- what progress they want
- what alternative you must beat
- why your approach is different
- what proof makes the promise believable

Treat the value proposition as a hypothesis until customers understand it, repeat it back in their own language, compare it to the expected alternative, and show pull.

## Why It Matters
A weak value proposition makes customers work too hard to understand why they should care. Strong positioning helps the right customer quickly see: this is for me, it solves a problem I have, it beats what I do today, and I believe the claim.

The strongest early positioning starts with customer evidence and real alternatives, not founder-preferred features. If the alternative, customer, or proof is wrong, polished copy only makes the wrong idea sound cleaner.

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this guide, founders should be able to:
- Use customer language to describe the job, pain, desired outcome, and buying trigger.
- Map jobs, pains, and gains to pain relievers, gain creators, differentiated capabilities, and proof.
- Translate features into customer value using "so what?" and "compared to what?" tests.
- Choose a familiar category or frame that improves comprehension without creating false expectations.
- Produce the guide deliverable: a positioning hypothesis, value map, differentiated value points, evidence labels, and clarity-test plan.

## Research Standard
Every part of the value proposition should be labeled by evidence quality:
- **Observed:** seen in customer behavior, workflow review, usage data, or current process evidence.
- **Quoted:** stated directly by a customer or prospect.
- **Inferred:** reasonable interpretation from multiple signals, but not directly confirmed.
- **Assumed:** founder belief that still needs validation.

Do not hide assumptions inside polished language. The purpose of this guide is to make the promise specific enough to test.

## Recommended Exercise
### 01. Pull Forward the Core Inputs
Bring in evidence from earlier guides and label each input as observed, quoted, inferred, or assumed.

| Input | Source guide | What to capture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ICP or beachhead customer | Guide 1 | The specific customer segment the promise is for |
| Painful job or problem | Guide 2 | The progress customers are trying to make and what blocks it |
| Interview patterns | Guide 3 | Customer quotes, repeated language, and common moments of pain |
| Current alternatives | Guide 4 | Status quo cost, switching friction, and what customers use today |

### 02. Write the Customer Job in Progress Language
Describe what the customer is trying to accomplish, not just what feature they might want.

Use this structure:

> When [situation], [customer] wants to [make progress], but [constraint/pain] gets in the way.

Capture three kinds of progress when relevant:

| Progress type | What it includes |
| --- | --- |
| Functional | Save time, reduce errors, increase revenue, avoid risk, complete a workflow |
| Emotional | Feel confident, reduce anxiety, avoid frustration |
| Social | Look competent, protect status, avoid blame, gain trust internally |

**Weak:** "They need a better dashboard."

**Stronger:** "When the VP Ops prepares the Monday forecast, she wants to spot fulfillment risks before customers complain, but the data is spread across three systems and takes four hours to reconcile."

### 03. Extract Customer Language
Review interview notes and highlight the phrases customers actually used. Organize them by what the phrase reveals.

| Look for language about | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Trigger situation | Shows when the problem becomes urgent |
| Current workaround | Reveals the alternative you must beat |
| Painful moment | Makes the promise concrete |
| Cost of the current process | Connects the pain to money, time, risk, or effort |
| Better outcome | Shows what customers actually want the product to make possible |
| Urgency or emotion | Surfaces risk, embarrassment, frustration, or success language |

Prefer customer wording over founder wording. If customers say "month-end fire drill," do not translate it into "financial workflow optimization" unless the buyer uses that language too.

Useful quote patterns include:

| Quote pattern | What it can reveal |
| --- | --- |
| "Every time we..." | Recurring trigger |
| "The worst part is..." | Acute pain |
| "We currently use..." | Current alternative |
| "I know this is working when..." | Success criteria |
| "I would switch if..." | Adoption threshold |
| "I don't believe you can..." | Believability gap |

### 04. Map Customer Value
Use the Value Proposition Canvas as a research translation tool, not a static worksheet.

| Canvas area | What to document |
| --- | --- |
| Jobs | What the customer is trying to get done or become better at |
| Pains | Costs, risks, frustrations, delays, errors, anxiety, political friction, or switching barriers |
| Gains | Desired outcomes, wins, status improvements, speed, control, confidence, revenue, savings, or reduced effort |
| Product/services | What your product, service, workflow, or manual concierge process provides |
| Pain relievers | How your approach reduces specific pains |
| Gain creators | How your approach creates specific gains |

Each pain reliever or gain creator should connect to a real customer pain or gain. If it does not, mark it as an assumption or remove it.

### 05. Name the Real Competitive Alternative
Ask: **If this product disappeared, what would the customer use tomorrow?**

Include non-obvious alternatives, not just direct competitors.

| Alternative type | What to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Manual process | Repeated human effort, handoffs, or review cycles |
| Spreadsheet or internal tool | Existing workaround with familiar ownership |
| Employee time | Work absorbed by a team member or function |
| Agency, consultant, or outsourced labor | External service replacing software adoption |
| Incumbent platform | Tool already approved, integrated, or budgeted |
| Direct competitor | Product solving a similar problem |
| Budget tradeoff | Another priority competing for the same money |
| Doing nothing | Status quo that feels safer than switching |

Document why the alternative persists. It may be trusted, already budgeted, politically safe, compliant enough, familiar, integrated, or simply good enough.

### 06. List Unique Attributes Relative to the Alternative
Follow Dunford's sequence by listing attributes only after the alternative is clear.

Use this prompt:

> Compared with [alternative], we can uniquely [capability].

Cut claims that are not specific until they are translated into concrete customer value and proof.

| Vague claim | What it must become |
| --- | --- |
| AI-powered | The specific decision, task, or workflow AI improves |
| All-in-one | The tools, handoffs, or duplicate work it removes |
| Seamless | The specific friction that disappears |
| Modern | The outdated limitation it replaces |
| Better UX | The measurable effort, confusion, or error reduction |
| Next-gen | The new capability customers can verify |
| Platform | The connected workflow or system of record customers need |

### 07. Translate Attributes into Value and Proof
For each candidate value point, capture the logic from pain to proof.

| Field | Prompt |
| --- | --- |
| Pain | What customer pain does this address? |
| Differentiated capability | What does your product do differently from the alternative? |
| Value | Why does the customer care? |
| Proof | Why should they believe it? |

Use this structure:

> Because we [capability], [ICP] can [outcome], which matters because [business/personal impact]. We believe this because [evidence].

**Feature list:** "Automated reconciliation, Slack alerts, AI summaries."

**Value and proof:** "Because the system reconciles order data across Shopify, NetSuite, and the 3PL automatically, RevOps can find fulfillment risks before Monday's executive meeting instead of spending four hours in spreadsheets. We believe this because three interviewed teams described the same weekly reconciliation process and one pilot file showed 14 preventable exceptions."

### 08. Choose the Category or Familiar Frame
Category helps customers understand what box to put the product in. It shapes expectations about competitors, price, required features, risk, and buying process.

Ask these questions before choosing the frame:

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| What familiar category or frame helps the customer understand this fastest? | Reduces comprehension work |
| What alternative does that frame imply? | Shapes the comparison set |
| What wrong assumptions might the frame create? | Prevents avoidable confusion |
| Does the category make the product sound bigger, smaller, riskier, or more familiar than it should? | Checks whether the frame helps or hurts adoption |

If the category is new, start with an adjacent familiar frame. Do not force customers to learn a new category before they understand the value.

### 09. Draft the Positioning Hypothesis
Use this primary template:

> For [specific ICP] who [painful job/problem], [product] is a [category/approach] that helps them [measurable or observable outcome]. Unlike [current alternative], it [differentiated capability/value], because [reason to believe/proof].

The Crossing the Chasm positioning statement is also useful for early B2B technology markets:

> For [target customer] who [need/opportunity], [product] is a [category] that [compelling reason to buy]. Unlike [primary competitive alternative], [product] [primary differentiation].

Use whichever version forces the sharper thinking. The exact wording matters less than whether the statement names the customer, need, category, alternative, differentiation, and reason to believe.

### 10. Write 3-5 Clear Value Points
Each value point should include the full chain from customer pain to proof.

| Required element | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Customer pain | The specific problem the customer recognizes |
| Alternative it beats | What the customer uses or does today |
| Differentiated capability | What your product does differently |
| Customer outcome | The result the customer gets |
| Proof or evidence label | Why the claim is believable and how strong the evidence is |

**Weak value point:** "Easy-to-use AI dashboard."

**Stronger value point:** "Compared with exporting three reports into a spreadsheet, the product flags fulfillment exceptions automatically, so Ops leaders can review risk in 10 minutes before the Monday forecast meeting. Evidence: quoted by four interviewees as a recurring workflow; timing still assumed."

**Weak positioning hypothesis:** "We are an AI-powered operations platform for ecommerce brands."

**Stronger positioning hypothesis:** "For ecommerce Ops leaders who prepare weekly fulfillment forecasts, FulfillSight is an operations-risk workspace that helps them identify late-order risk before customers complain. Unlike spreadsheet reconciliation across Shopify, NetSuite, and 3PL exports, it flags exceptions automatically and shows the source record, because it connects the three systems teams already reconcile by hand."

### 11. Run Internal Stress Tests
Before testing with customers, run three checks.

| Test | How to use it |
| --- | --- |
| So what? | If you name a feature, ask "so what?" until it becomes customer value tied to pain, money, speed, risk, status, or effort. |
| Compared to what? | Every claim should name the alternative it beats. |
| Say it back | Someone unfamiliar with the product should be able to repeat who it is for, what it does, why it matters, and why it is different. |

A strong output should pass eight quality checks:

| Quality | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Specific | Names a clear customer, problem, and situation |
| Understandable | Uses language customers can repeat |
| Customer-language-based | Reflects words customers actually use |
| Alternative-aware | Makes the comparison explicit |
| Outcome-oriented | Describes progress, not just features |
| Differentiated | Explains why this approach is meaningfully different |
| Believable | Includes evidence or a reason to believe |
| Testable | Can be validated or disproven in customer conversations |

### 12. Test the Promise in 5-10 Customer Conversations
Do not pitch for approval. Test for clarity, relevance, believability, and pull.

| Question | What it tests |
| --- | --- |
| "What do you think this means?" | Basic comprehension |
| "Who do you think this is for?" | ICP clarity |
| "What would you compare this to?" | Alternative and category frame |
| "Which part feels most valuable?" | Value priority |
| "Which part feels least believable?" | Proof gap |
| "What would need to be true for this to be worth trying?" | Adoption threshold |
| "What words would you use to describe this internally?" | Customer language |
| "Who else would need to care?" | Stakeholder map |

Watch for pull signals during the conversation.

| Pull signal | What it suggests |
| --- | --- |
| They repeat the value in their own words | The message is understandable |
| They compare it to the expected alternative | The frame is landing correctly |
| They describe a current workflow where it would fit | The pain is active |
| They ask to see it | Interest is moving beyond politeness |
| They ask about price, timing, implementation, security, or next steps | Buying criteria are emerging |
| They introduce another stakeholder | Internal relevance is expanding |
| They offer data, access, or a pilot opportunity | They are investing effort |

If customers compare it to a different alternative than expected, revisit the positioning. Their frame of reference matters more than the founder's preferred category.

## Guide Deliverable
Create a concise positioning deliverable with these sections:

| Section | Required content |
| --- | --- |
| Positioning hypothesis | One sentence using the template in this guide |
| Customer-language evidence | 5-10 quotes or phrases from interviews that describe the job, pain, alternative, desired outcome, or urgency |
| Value map summary | Jobs, pains, gains, pain relievers, and gain creators |
| Differentiated value points | 3-5 value points, each with customer pain, alternative, differentiated capability, outcome, proof, and evidence label |
| Category/frame decision | Chosen category or familiar frame, why it helps, and wrong assumptions to avoid |
| Test plan | 5-10 target customers, questions to ask, expected alternative, pull signals, and assumptions to validate before moving forward |
