## Field Brief
Run discovery interviews to test your customer, problem, urgency, alternative, and buying-context assumptions. The goal is not to pitch the product or collect compliments. The goal is to learn how target customers experience the problem today, what they already do about it, what it costs, and what would justify moving forward.

A good discovery interview is a hypothesis test: write down what you believe before the call, ask about real past behavior during the call, and update your assumptions afterward based on evidence.

## Why It Matters
Customer interviews protect founders from building on guesses, politeness, and hypotheticals. Strong interviews reveal whether the problem is real, who feels it most, what language customers use, what alternatives they already use, what triggers urgency, who controls the budget, and what would make someone change.

Bad interviews create false confidence. If you pitch too early, ask leading questions, or accept feature requests as instructions, you may mistake interest for demand. Strong discovery keeps the conversation anchored in the customer's current reality.

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this guide, founders should be able to:
- Turn assumptions into a focused discovery plan.
- Conduct story-based interviews about recent real behavior.
- Probe alternatives, cost, urgency, buying context, and switching triggers.
- Separate evidence from compliments, hypotheticals, and feature requests.
- Produce a discovery plan, script, and evidence log from real customer conversations.

## Recommended Exercise
### 01. Write the discovery plan before scheduling calls
Document the assumptions these interviews are meant to test.

| Assumption | What to define |
| --- | --- |
| Target ICP | The narrow customer segment you believe has the problem. |
| Problem | The painful situation you believe they experience. |
| Current alternative | How you believe they solve, work around, or avoid it today. |
| Urgency | Why this problem matters now, not someday. |
| Cost | What the problem costs in time, money, risk, lost revenue, delay, churn, compliance exposure, reputation, or frustration. |
| Buyer | Who uses the solution, champions it, approves it, and pays for it. |
| Trigger | What event makes the customer actively look for a better answer. |

For each assumption, write what evidence would strengthen it, weaken it, or invalidate it. Decide what product or market decision this interview batch will inform.

### 02. Build a high-quality interview target list
Create a list of 25-50 people or companies from your ICP. For each target, capture qualification details in a simple table.

| Field | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Name or account | Identifies the person or company you want to reach. |
| Role or company type | Confirms whether they match the ICP. |
| Contact path | Shows how you can reach them. |
| ICP fit evidence | Explains why they appear to match the segment. |
| Recent problem exposure | Increases the odds that the interview produces concrete stories. |
| Buying-context role | Marks them as user, champion, buyer, blocker, or expert referrer. |
| Urgency signal | Captures any evidence that the problem may matter now. |

Prioritize targets with recent exposure to the problem, operational responsibility, budget influence, or access to the buyer. For B2B, include both users and buyers when possible. A broad list produces scattered data; a narrow list produces patterns.

### 03. Write outreach that asks for advice, not a pitch meeting
The outreach should make the person feel useful, not sold to. Keep it short and specific.

| Outreach element | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Who you are | Gives enough context to make the request credible. |
| Problem area | Frames the research topic without pitching a solution. |
| Why they are relevant | Shows the request is specific, not mass outreach. |
| 20-30 minute advice ask | Makes the commitment clear and lightweight. |
| No pitch or demo | Reduces defensiveness and bias. |

Example structure:
> I'm researching how [specific type of person/team] handles [problem area]. You seem close to this because [specific reason]. Would you be open to a 20-30 minute call? I'm not selling anything; I'm trying to understand the current workflow and where it breaks.

### 04. Open the call in a way that reduces bias
Start by making it clear that honest detail is more useful than encouragement.

**Opening language**
> I'm not here to sell anything today. I'm trying to understand how people currently handle [problem area]. Please do not worry about being polite; specifics about what actually happens are more useful than positive feedback.

**Setup checklist**
- Ask permission to record or take notes.
- Confirm how much time they have.
- Start with their role, workflow, and context before discussing the problem.

### 05. Use a story-based interview flow
Anchor the interview on a real recent incident instead of a general opinion.

| Interview move | Example question |
| --- | --- |
| Find the incident | "Tell me about the last time this happened." |
| Locate the trigger | "What triggered it?" |
| Reconstruct the sequence | "What happened first? What happened next?" |
| Map the people involved | "Who else was involved?" |
| Find the pain | "Where did it get hard?" |
| Reveal the workaround | "What did you do to solve or work around it?" |
| Close the story | "What happened in the end?" |

When the customer speaks generally, move them back to a concrete example.

| If they generalize... | Pull them back with... |
| --- | --- |
| "Usually..." | "Can you walk me through the most recent time?" |
| "We just handle it manually." | "Can you show me what that looked like?" |
| "Then we follow up." | "What did you actually do next?" |

### 06. Probe current alternatives and cost
Strong problems usually leave evidence. Current alternatives are not a side note; they show that the problem is real and that the customer has already made tradeoffs.

| Discovery area | Questions to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Current workaround | What tools, spreadsheets, agencies, consultants, people, processes, or hacks are involved today? |
| Prior attempts | What have they already tried, and what did they dislike about it? |
| Frequency | How often does the problem happen? |
| Time cost | How long does it take to deal with? |
| Business cost | What breaks if they do not solve it? |
| Cost categories | What does it cost in money, time, risk, lost revenue, churn, delay, compliance exposure, or reputation? |
| Staying power | What would make the current workaround good enough to keep? |

### 07. Capture B2B buying context where relevant
For company customers, the person with the pain may not be the person with budget authority. Keep discovery connected to eventual sales reality.

| Buying-context field | What to capture |
| --- | --- |
| Roles | User, champion, economic buyer, decision-maker, and blocker. |
| Budget | Budget source and business case required. |
| Approval path | Decision process, procurement, security, legal, or compliance constraints. |
| Timing | What would create urgency this quarter. |

A painful workflow without a buyer, budget, or business case may not become a market.

### 08. Treat feature requests as clues, not instructions
Customers are often better at explaining problems than designing solutions. If someone asks for a feature, do not add it directly to the roadmap. Translate the request into the underlying job, pain, constraint, or buying trigger.

| Probe | What it reveals |
| --- | --- |
| "What would that help you accomplish?" | The job behind the requested feature. |
| "What happens today because you do not have that?" | The pain or consequence. |
| "How do you handle that now?" | The current workaround. |
| "When was the last time you needed it?" | Whether the need is recent and real. |
| "How important is that compared with the rest of the problem?" | Relative priority. |

### 09. Avoid weak questions
Use past behavior instead of future opinion.

| Weak question | Better question |
| --- | --- |
| "Would you use this?" | "How do you solve this today?" |
| "Would you pay for this?" | "What does this problem cost you today?" |
| "Do you like this idea?" | "Tell me about the last time this problem came up." |
| "What features do you want?" | "What were you trying to accomplish when you needed that?" |

### 10. End with evidence and advancement
Before ending the call, ask for evidence and a next step.

**Evidence questions**
- "Is there anything I should have asked but didn't?"
- "Could you share an example, screenshot, template, report, spreadsheet, ticket, invoice, email thread, or workflow output?"
- "Who else should I talk to?"
- "Who owns this problem or budget internally?"

If the pain is strong, ask for a meaningful advancement. Compliments are not advancement; a useful next commitment shows that the problem matters enough for the customer to spend more time, social capital, data access, or money.

| Stage | Possible advancement |
| --- | --- |
| More discovery | Second call, intro to the buyer, or intro to another user. |
| Workflow evidence | Permission to observe the workflow or review relevant work samples. |
| Solution shaping | Participation in a design review or advisory involvement. |
| Commercial signal | Pilot interest, letter of intent, pre-order, or paid test. |

### 11. Capture evidence after every interview
Update your interview log immediately after each call.

| Evidence field | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Customer language | Exact customer phrases and emotional intensity. |
| Segment context | Segment, role, and whether the person is user, champion, buyer, decision-maker, or blocker. |
| Problem strength | Pain intensity, frequency, trigger event, and cost of the problem. |
| Current behavior | Workaround, alternatives tried, and work samples requested or received. |
| Sales reality | Objections, constraints, and buying-context notes. |
| Interpretation | Feature requests translated into underlying pains and founder notes on changed assumptions. |
| Advancement | Next commitment or lack of commitment. |

Then tag each interview so patterns are easier to see.

| Tag group | Tags |
| --- | --- |
| Pain strength | Strong pain, moderate pain, weak pain. |
| ICP signal | Strong ICP, wrong ICP, potential earlyvangelist. |
| Buying role | Buyer, user, champion, blocker. |

### 12. Synthesize patterns across interviews
Do not change direction after one interesting conversation. Review patterns after every 5 interviews and again after at least 10 interviews in one narrow segment.

Look for repeated evidence across the batch.

| Pattern type | What to compare |
| --- | --- |
| Pain pattern | Same painful trigger, workflow breakdown, or urgency source. |
| Behavior pattern | Same current workaround or repeated alternative. |
| Language pattern | Same words, phrases, or framing. |
| Business pattern | Same cost, consequence, buyer, or approval path. |
| Commitment pattern | Same objections or same meaningful next commitment. |

If answers are scattered, the ICP is probably too broad. Narrow the segment and run another batch.

### 13. Apply the do-not-move-forward gate
Do not move forward to solution validation or product building if most evidence is weak.

| Warning signal | What it suggests |
| --- | --- |
| Vague or hypothetical problem descriptions | The pain may not be real or frequent enough. |
| Few recent concrete examples | The problem may not be active. |
| No existing workaround or prior attempt | The customer may not care enough to act. |
| Low or unclear cost | The business case may be too weak. |
| No urgent trigger | The problem may be easy to postpone. |
| Unknown buyer, budget, or decision path | The pain may not translate into a market. |
| Compliments without next commitments | Interest is not strong enough yet. |
| No repeated pattern inside a narrow segment | The ICP or problem definition is probably too broad. |

Move forward only when a narrow segment repeatedly shows real pain, current alternatives, meaningful cost, clear triggers, and enough commitment to justify the next validation step.

## Guide Deliverable
Submit a completed discovery package.

| Deliverable component | Required contents |
| --- | --- |
| Discovery plan | ICP, assumptions, evidence criteria, and the decision this batch informs. |
| Target list | 25-50 qualified interview prospects. |
| Outreach message | Advice-oriented copy, not a pitch or demo request. |
| Interview script | Bias-reducing opening and story-based interview flow. |
| Completed interviews | At least 10 customer interviews in one narrow segment. |
| Evidence log | Exact phrases, pain intensity, frequency, workaround, cost, trigger, buyer context, objections, work samples, and next commitment. |
| Synthesis summary | Repeated patterns, strongest segment, strongest pain, current alternatives, buying context, and recommended next step. |
| Do-not-move-forward assessment | Whether the evidence is strong enough for the next PMF step. |
