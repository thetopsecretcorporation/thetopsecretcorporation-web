## Brief
Discovery interviews test assumptions about the customer, pain, alternatives, urgency, cost, and buying context. The goal is not to pitch the product or collect compliments. The goal is to learn what customers already do, what it costs, and what would justify change.

Steve Blank's rule still holds: "There are no facts inside the building so get the heck outside." But outside only helps if the conversation is grounded in behavior. Ask about recent stories, current workflows, prior attempts, and actual commitments.

Use the ICP from Guide 01 as the filter, and keep MVP questions separate from discovery questions. In business-to-business discovery, the person who feels the pain may not approve budget.

The Mom Test frames the risk well: customer conversations should help you get more learning "even when everyone is lying to you." Most customers are not trying to mislead you; they are being polite, speculative, or solution-happy. Your job is to listen for evidence.

## Exercise
Build the Discovery Evidence Pack while running interviews.

### 01. Write the discovery plan
Before scheduling calls, document what this batch is meant to test:

- Target Ideal Customer Profile.
- Painful problem.
- Current alternative or workaround.
- Urgency and trigger event.

- Cost in time, money, risk, lost revenue, delay, churn, compliance exposure, reputation, or stress.
- User, champion, buyer, decision-maker, and blocker.
- The decision this interview batch will inform.
- What evidence would strengthen, weaken, or invalidate each assumption.

A broad plan produces scattered anecdotes. A narrow plan produces patterns.

### 02. Build the target list and outreach
Create a list of 25-50 qualified people or companies from one narrow segment. Prioritize targets with recent exposure to the problem, operational responsibility, budget influence, or access to the buyer.

Track:

- Name or account.
- Role or company type.
- Contact path.
- Evidence they fit the segment.

- Recent problem exposure.
- Buying-context role.
- Urgency signal.

Write outreach that asks for advice, not a pitch meeting:

> I'm researching how [specific type of person/team] handles [problem area]. You seem close to this because [specific reason]. Would you be open to a 20-30 minute call? I'm not selling anything; I'm trying to understand the current workflow and where it breaks.

You can always reach back out to these people later if they're a good fit for your product or service.

### 03. Use a story-based script
Open the call in a way that reduces bias:

> I'm not here to sell anything today. I'm trying to understand how people currently handle [problem area]. Please do not worry about being polite; specifics about what actually happens are more useful than positive feedback.

Then anchor on real behavior:

- "Tell me about the last time this happened."
- "What triggered it?"
- "What happened first? What happened next?"
- "Who else was involved?"

- "How do you solve this today?"
- "Can you show me what that looked like?"
- "What does this cost in time, money, risk, or missed opportunity?"
- "Why haven't you changed already?"

Avoid weak questions like "Would you use this?" and "Would you pay for this?" They invite guesses. Ask what they already do, what they already spend, and what already breaks.

### 04. Probe alternatives, cost, urgency, and buying path
Strong problems leave evidence. Look for:

- Current tools, spreadsheets, agencies, consultants, people, manual processes, or hacks.
- Prior attempts and why they failed.
- Frequency and recency.
- Time cost and business cost.

- Trigger events that make the problem urgent this quarter.
- Budget source, approval path, procurement, security, legal, or compliance constraints.
- Current objections and switching fears.

Treat feature requests as clues, not roadmap instructions. Ask what the request would help them accomplish, what happens today without it, and when they last needed it.

### 05. End with evidence and advancement
At the end of your interview, if there's sufficient evidence this customer may be open to a solution, you can try to advance the conversation to a pitch.

Advancement means a concrete next commitment: a second call, buyer intro, shared data, workflow sample, design review, pilot scope, letter of intent, pre-order, or paid test.

Before ending the call, ask:

- "Is there anything I should have asked but didn't?"
- "Could you share an example, screenshot, template, report, spreadsheet, ticket, invoice, email thread, or workflow output?"
- "Who else should I talk to?"
- "Who owns this problem or budget internally?"

Compliments are not advancement. A next commitment shows the problem matters enough for the customer to spend time, social capital, data access, or money.

### 06. Capture evidence immediately
After every interview, update the evidence log:

- Exact customer language.
- Segment, role, and buying-context position.
- Pain intensity, frequency, trigger, and cost.
- Current behavior, workaround, alternatives tried, and work samples.

- Objections, constraints, and buying-context notes.
- Feature requests translated into underlying pain.
- Advancement or lack of advancement.
- What changed in the team's assumptions.

Tag each interview for strong/moderate/weak pain, strong/wrong ICP fit, buyer/user/champion/blocker, and meaningful/no advancement.

### 07. Synthesize after 5-10 interviews
Do not change direction after one interesting call. Review patterns after every 5 interviews and again after at least 10 interviews in one narrow segment.

Look for repeated evidence:

- Same painful trigger or workflow breakdown.
- Same current workaround or alternative.
- Same customer language.
- Same cost, consequence, buyer, or approval path.

- Same objections or same next commitment.

If answers are scattered, the segment is probably too broad. Narrow the Ideal Customer Profile and run another batch.

## Deliverable
Create a **Discovery Evidence Pack** and keep it visible to the founding team and key employees. It should become the shared source of truth for what customers actually said and did.

Include:

- Discovery plan.
- Qualified target list.
- Advice-oriented outreach message.
- Story-based interview script.

- At least 10 completed interviews in one narrow segment.
- Evidence log with exact phrases, pain, frequency, workaround, cost, trigger, buyer context, objections, artifacts, and next commitments.
- Pattern summary.
- Recommendation: proceed, narrow the segment, revise the problem, or stop.

## Exit Criteria
Move on when:

- At least 10 interviews are completed in one narrow segment.
- Notes include exact customer language.
- Every strong claim ties to a recent customer story.

- Alternatives and workarounds are documented.
- Cost and urgency are documented.
- Buying roles are identified where relevant.
