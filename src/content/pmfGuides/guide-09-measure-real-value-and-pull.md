## Field Brief
Measure whether customers are getting value and pulling the product forward before you scale. Separate real PMF evidence from vanity metrics, compliments, one-time curiosity, and founder-led momentum that will not repeat.

Use three evidence layers: survey dependence, behavioral value, and market pull. No single signal proves PMF. The job is to triangulate what customers say, what they do, and what they are willing to commit.

## Why It Matters
PMF shows up in behavior. Customers come back, pay, refer others, expand usage, tolerate rough edges, and would be upset if the product disappeared. If users do not retain or pull, the founder should not scale yet.

Top-line growth can lie. Signups, press, demo enthusiasm, free trials, page views, and compliments can look encouraging while the product fails to become part of the customer's real workflow. Retention is harder to fake because it asks whether the customer keeps receiving value after the novelty is gone.

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this guide, founders should be able to:
- Define the core value event and natural usage frequency.
- Distinguish real PMF signals from vanity metrics and false positives.
- Use the Sean Ellis survey as a directional dependence signal without overclaiming it.
- Segment retention and survey evidence to find the highest-pull customer group.
- Produce the guide deliverable: a PMF scorecard with behavior, survey evidence, revenue or commitment, pull, and a clear stage decision.

## Recommended Exercise
### 01. Define the value event
Before looking at dashboards, define what value actually means.

| Field | What to document |
| --- | --- |
| **Promised outcome** | What customer outcome did the product promise to create? |
| **Value event** | What observable behavior proves the customer received that outcome? |
| **First value moment** | What is the earliest point where the customer can experience real value? |
| **Repeat value behavior** | What should the customer do again if the product is working? |
| **Natural frequency** | Should this happen daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, or only when a trigger event occurs? |

Avoid vanity definitions such as signups, visits, logins, idle sessions, demo attendance, generic clicks, or feature exploration unless they directly represent delivered value.

### 02. Measure behavioral value
Track usage around the value event, not around generic activity.

| Measurement | What it should reveal |
| --- | --- |
| **Activation into first value** | Whether customers reach the first meaningful outcome. |
| **Time to first value** | How quickly the product starts delivering value. |
| **Repeat value events** | Whether the value event happens again at the natural cadence. |
| **Cohort retention** | Whether customers continue receiving value after novelty fades. |
| **Core workflow depth** | Whether the product is becoming part of the job, not just being explored. |
| **Team or account adoption** | Whether usage spreads to teammates or across the customer account. |
| **Paid conversion, renewal, or expansion** | Whether customers are willing to commit money or budget. |

For retention, define "retained" as repeating the value event at the expected cadence. A daily workflow product should show frequent engagement quickly. A monthly or quarterly workflow needs a longer observation window. The retention metric only means something if it matches how often the customer naturally has the job.

Create cohorts by week or month, depending on usage frequency. Then ask:
- What percentage of each cohort keeps performing the value event over time?
- Does the retention curve flatten for any segment?
- Which customers are retained, and what do they have in common?
- Are retained users also the users who report high dependence?

If the curve trends toward zero, signups and compliments are noise.

### 03. Measure customer dependence
The Sean Ellis survey asks active users:

> How would you feel if you could no longer use this product?

| Answer option | How to interpret it |
| --- | --- |
| **Very disappointed** | Strong dependence signal when the user is qualified. |
| **Somewhat disappointed** | Useful, but usually needs segmentation and product improvement. |
| **Not disappointed** | Weak dependence; investigate whether the user ever reached value. |
| **No longer using it** | Churn signal; compare against retained behavior. |

The common benchmark is roughly **40% "very disappointed"** among qualified users. Treat this as a useful operating signal, not a universal law or standalone proof of PMF.

Survey only users who have experienced the product's core value, used it more than once, and used it recently enough to answer from real experience. Surveying unactivated users, freebie hunters, superfans only, or people who have not reached the value event will distort the result.

Ask follow-up questions:
- What type of person would benefit most from this product?
- What is the main benefit you receive?
- How can we improve it for you?
- What would you use if this product disappeared?

The follow-ups matter as much as the percentage. They reveal who depends on the product, what value they perceive, and which alternatives you are replacing.

### 04. Segment for the high-expectation customer
The Superhuman PMF Engine is useful because it does not stop at the aggregate survey score. It asks founders to identify the segment that feels the strongest dependence, then shape the product and positioning around that group.

Break results out across these lenses:

| Lens | What to compare |
| --- | --- |
| **Customer fit** | ICP fit, role, persona, company size, and customer type. |
| **Use case** | The job, workflow, or trigger event the product supports. |
| **Source and path** | Acquisition source and activation path. |
| **Commitment level** | Paying status, renewal, expansion, or other commitment evidence. |
| **Behavioral outcome** | Frequency of value events and retained vs. churned users. |

Then document the patterns that matter most:

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Who says "very disappointed"? | Identifies the strongest stated dependence. |
| Who repeats the value event? | Confirms dependence with behavior. |
| Who pays, renews, expands, refers, or invites teammates? | Shows commitment and market pull. |
| What do the highest-pull users have in common? | Defines the segment to focus on. |
| What words do they use to describe the main benefit? | Improves positioning and customer language. |
| Which "somewhat disappointed" users could become very disappointed if the product improved one specific thing? | Reveals the most productive improvement path. |

Your strongest segment is not necessarily your largest segment. It is the narrow group where dependence, retained behavior, and pull reinforce one another.

### 05. Separate pull signals from weak signals
Strong pull means the customer does extra work to keep using, expand, or share the product.

| Stronger pull signal | What it indicates |
| --- | --- |
| Customers ask to use the product again. | The product is solving a real recurring need. |
| Users invite teammates without being pushed. | Value is spreading inside the account. |
| Customers refer peers or make introductions. | The product is creating enough value to share. |
| Prospects ask for access, pricing, or implementation details. | Buying intent is becoming more concrete. |
| Customers pay, renew, expand, or commit budget. | Value is strong enough to justify commitment. |
| Users tolerate rough edges because the outcome matters. | The pain or desired outcome is important enough to overcome friction. |
| Sales conversations get easier or shorter. | The market understands the promise faster. |
| The product enters the customer's normal workflow. | Usage is becoming durable instead of episodic. |
| Champions push internally for adoption. | The customer is doing work on the product's behalf. |

| Weak or misleading signal | Why it can fool founders |
| --- | --- |
| Signups without value-event usage. | Interest does not prove delivered value. |
| Compliments without behavior. | Praise is cheaper than adoption. |
| Demo enthusiasm that never converts. | Curiosity can look like demand. |
| Press, launch spikes, or social attention. | Visibility can fade without retention. |
| Free users who never commit. | Usage without commitment may not reflect urgency. |
| Incentivized referrals. | The incentive may be stronger than the product pull. |
| NPS without retention. | Sentiment is weak if customers do not come back. |
| Many different favorite features with no clear pattern. | The product may lack a focused value proposition. |
| One large bespoke customer pulling the roadmap away from the target market. | Revenue can distract from repeatable PMF. |
| Founder-led handholding that cannot repeat. | The service layer may be carrying the product. |
| Top-line growth with poor retention. | Acquisition can hide a leaky product. |

### 06. Build the PMF scorecard
Create a simple scorecard with three layers of evidence, then add one final line: **decision and rationale**.

| Evidence layer | Include |
| --- | --- |
| **Stated dependence** | Qualified survey response count, percent "very disappointed," strongest "very disappointed" segment, main benefit in the customer's own words, and the most common alternative if the product disappeared. |
| **Behavioral value** | Defined value event, expected usage frequency, activation into first value, time to first value, repeat value-event rate, cohort retention, and depth of workflow or team adoption. |
| **Market pull and commitment** | Paid conversion, renewal or expansion, referrals or customer-initiated intros, inbound requests, sales urgency or shorter sales cycles, willingness to tolerate rough edges, and top qualitative pull quotes. |
| **Decision and rationale** | A clear call on whether to continue, narrow, iterate, or avoid moving forward to scale. |

### 07. Make the stage decision
Use the scorecard to decide what happens next.

| Decision | Use it when | Next move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Continue** | The best-fit segment shows strong qualitative dependence, repeat value behavior, and early pull. | Keep deepening the product for that segment. |
| **Narrow** | One segment loves the product and others are weak. | Tighten the ICP, promise, onboarding, and roadmap around the high-pull segment. |
| **Iterate** | There is some signal, but it is inconsistent. | Decide whether the next change is to the persona, problem, promise, or product. |
| **Do not move forward to scale** | Usage is shallow, cohort retention decays toward zero, the "very disappointed" score is weak or unqualified, and customers are not paying, renewing, referring, or pulling the product into their workflow. | Return to the earlier PMF guides instead of adding acquisition spend, hiring sales, or building a larger roadmap. |

First Round's PMF levels can help founders avoid a false binary:
- **Nascent:** A few customers show real dependence, but repeatability is not proven.
- **Developing:** A clear segment retains and demand is becoming more repeatable.
- **Strong:** Satisfaction, demand, and delivery efficiency reinforce one another.
- **Extreme:** The market is pulling product from the company faster than the team can comfortably keep up.

Most early teams are looking for nascent or developing evidence, not permission to declare victory.

## Guide Deliverable
A PMF scorecard that includes the core evidence needed to make a clear stage decision.

| Deliverable section | Required content |
| --- | --- |
| **Value definition** | The promised outcome, core value event, and natural usage frequency. |
| **Behavioral evidence** | Activation, time-to-value, repeat usage, and cohort retention tied to the value event. |
| **Survey evidence** | Sean Ellis PMF survey results with qualified-user caveats. |
| **Segment insight** | The highest-pull segment or high-expectation customer profile. |
| **Customer language** | Qualitative pull quotes and customer wording. |
| **Commitment evidence** | Revenue, renewal, expansion, referral, or other commitment signals. |
| **False positives** | Weak signals and misleading evidence to ignore. |
| **Stage decision** | A continue, narrow, iterate, or do-not-move-forward decision with rationale. |
