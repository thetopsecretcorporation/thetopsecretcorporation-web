## Field Brief
Identify what customers do today when your product does not exist. Do not list every company you fear. Map the customer's real alternatives: manual work, spreadsheets, agencies, internal teams, incumbent platforms, direct competitors, delaying the decision, or doing nothing.

Customers always have an alternative. If they care about the problem, they have found some way to survive it. That alternative may be a person, a process, a budget tradeoff, a half-broken workaround, or the decision to tolerate the pain for another quarter.

## Why It Matters
The status quo is often the primary competitor. Customers keep current behavior because it is familiar, already budgeted, politically safe, integrated into workflows, compliant enough, or simply good enough. A better product does not automatically beat a trusted process.

Alternatives shape positioning, proof, product scope, pricing, and sales motion. If you guess the alternative wrong, your value proposition usually drifts with it.

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this guide, founders should be able to:

| Skill | What founders should produce |
| --- | --- |
| Separate real alternatives from phantom competitors | A list grounded in actual customer behavior, not founder anxiety |
| Understand why the status quo survives | Notes on what it does well, what it costs, and why customers keep using it |
| Map switching forces | A clear view of push, pull, anxiety, and habit around changing behavior |
| Identify proof requirements | The minimum evidence needed to make switching feel safe |
| Complete the guide deliverable | An alternative map, status quo cost statement, switching-risk statement, switching map, and minimum-proof plan |

## Research Standard
Only count an alternative as real if it appears in customer behavior. A real alternative is something customers use, pay for, recently tried, seriously evaluated, or explicitly say they will keep doing.

A phantom competitor is something founders worry about but customers do not mention, compare against, use, or budget for. Phantom competitors can be tracked as assumptions, but they should not drive positioning or product decisions until interviews confirm them.

If customers cannot name a current workaround, cannot remember the last time the problem happened, have never searched for a solution, and have no budget owner, do not treat them as high-intent early adopters yet.

## Recommended Exercise
### 01. Build a customer-view alternative inventory
Review interview notes and highlight every mention of current behavior. Capture the evidence in two passes: first by signal, then by alternative type.

| Signal to highlight | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| Current tool, process, workaround, spreadsheet, or manual process | How customers solve the problem today |
| Agency, contractor, internal team, incumbent platform, or direct competitor | Who or what currently absorbs the job |
| Delayed decision, doing nothing, or “we will just keep doing...” | Whether inertia is the real competitor |
| “We already use...” or “we tried...” | What customers have actually adopted or rejected |
| Budget owner or switching concern | Who must approve change and what could block it |

Then group the alternatives into buckets:

| Bucket | Use when customers rely on... |
| --- | --- |
| Do nothing / defer | Delay, tolerance, or quarterly “we’ll revisit this” behavior |
| Manual workaround | Repeated human effort, ad hoc processes, or copied-and-pasted fixes |
| Spreadsheet or internal tool | Homegrown systems, shared sheets, scripts, or internal dashboards |
| Incumbent platform or direct competitor | Existing software already in the workflow |
| Service, agency, contractor, or human labor | Outside help or internal teams doing the work manually |
| Budget tradeoff or competing internal priority | Another project, initiative, or owner competing for the same resources |

### 02. Interview for current behavior, not hypotheticals
Avoid questions like “Would you switch?”, “Would you pay?”, or “Do you like this idea?” They invite compliments and guesses.

Ask about past behavior and the current process instead:

| Interview area | Stronger questions |
| --- | --- |
| Current workflow | “How do you handle this today?” “Talk me through the last time this happened.” |
| Trigger and participants | “What triggered the workflow?” “Who was involved?” |
| System details | “What tools, people, approvals, and handoffs were required?” |
| Prior attempts | “What else have you tried?” “Why did you choose the current way?” |
| Value and pain | “What do you like about it?” “What breaks or frustrates you?” |
| Cost and resistance | “What does it cost in time, money, risk, or missed opportunity?” “Why haven’t you changed already?” |
| Approval and safety | “Who would need to approve a change?” “What would make switching feel safe?” |

### 03. Document each alternative
For each alternative, build a compact profile. Do not only document what is broken. The strengths of the current alternative become minimum expectations, objections, or switching requirements.

| Profile field | What to capture |
| --- | --- |
| Current behavior | What the customer does today, who performs the work, and how often it happens |
| Workflow mechanics | What triggers the workflow and what tools, people, approvals, and handoffs are involved |
| Ownership | Who owns the workflow and who owns the budget |
| Strengths | What the alternative does well and why customers keep using it |
| Weaknesses | What customers dislike and where the current process creates cost or risk |
| Viability bar | What the new product would have to preserve to be considered viable |

### 04. Quantify the cost of the current process
Capture status quo cost across multiple dimensions:

| Cost dimension | Examples to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Money | Software spend, agency fees, labor cost, contractor cost, implementation cost |
| Time | Hours per workflow, cycle time, waiting time, duplicated work |
| Risk | Errors, compliance exposure, missed deadlines, data loss, security concerns |
| Opportunity cost | Lost revenue, slower growth, fewer customers served, lower conversion |
| Emotional or social cost | Stress, embarrassment, internal blame, team frustration, executive pressure |

Doing nothing is still an alternative. It may signal low urgency, unclear ownership, lack of budget, fear of implementation, political risk, or a workaround that is painful but acceptable.

### 05. Build a Switching Map
Choose the highest-priority alternative and map the forces around switching:

| Force | Question to answer |
| --- | --- |
| Push | What makes the current way painful enough to motivate change? |
| Pull | What better future attracts the customer to a new approach? |
| Anxiety | What makes the new approach feel risky? |
| Habit | What behavior, workflow, contract, relationship, integration, or political comfort keeps the old way in place? |

Customers do not switch just because the new product is better. Switching happens when push and pull are strong enough to overcome anxiety and habit.

### 06. Identify the true early enemy
Name what your product must beat first. The true early enemy is the obstacle customers actually compare against when deciding whether to act.

| Enemy type | What it usually means |
| --- | --- |
| Direct competitor or incumbent platform | Customers already compare you to an existing tool |
| Spreadsheet, manual process, agency, contractor, or internal team | The workaround is inefficient but familiar enough to survive |
| No budget or no urgency | The pain exists, but it has not become a buying priority |
| No trust or fear of change | The customer needs proof before they will risk switching |
| Unclear owner | No one is accountable enough to sponsor the change |
| “Annoying but not worth solving now” | The problem is real, but the cost of action still feels higher than the cost of staying put |

### 07. Write the required statements
Write two concise statements that turn the research into usable positioning inputs.

| Statement | Template |
| --- | --- |
| Status quo cost statement | “Today, [ICP] solves [problem] by using [alternative], which costs them [specific cost/consequence].” |
| Switching-risk statement | “They do not switch because they fear [risk/anxiety] and are held in place by [habit/dependency].” |

Example statements:

> “Today, RevOps leaders solve territory planning by exporting CRM data into spreadsheets and manually reconciling account ownership, which costs two analysts 10-15 hours per month and creates errors before quarterly planning.”

> “They do not switch because they worry a new tool will require Salesforce cleanup, executive approval, and team retraining.”

### 08. Define the minimum proof needed to make switching safe
Choose the lowest-friction proof that could move a customer one step forward.

| Proof type | Best when the customer needs... |
| --- | --- |
| Workflow walkthrough or prototype | Confidence that the product fits their actual workflow |
| Manual concierge test or side-by-side benchmark | Evidence that the promised outcome is achievable |
| ROI calculation | A business case for budget approval |
| Migration plan or security/compliance documentation | Reduced implementation, IT, or risk anxiety |
| Customer reference or case study | Social proof that another similar customer succeeded |
| Free pilot, short paid pilot, or limited deployment | A low-risk way to test before a full commitment |
| Done-for-you onboarding or narrow first workflow | A wedge that avoids full rip-and-replace |

Early products usually win by finding a narrow wedge where the current alternative is weakest, the customer has urgency, and the proof burden is small enough to overcome.

### 09. Use alternatives to sharpen positioning
Use the map to complete this positioning sentence:

> “Compared with [real alternative], our product uniquely enables [capability], which creates [value] for [best-fit customer] in [high-urgency context].”

If this sentence cannot be written with evidence, continue discovery before moving forward.

## Guide Deliverable
Create one structured deliverable with these sections:

| Section | Required content |
| --- | --- |
| Alternative map | Evidence-based customer-view alternatives, separated from phantom competitors |
| Prioritized enemy | The status quo obstacle your product must beat first |
| Four Forces map | Push, pull, anxiety, and habit for the top alternative |
| Core statements | One status quo cost statement and one switching-risk statement |
| Minimum-proof plan | The lowest-friction evidence needed to make switching feel safe |
| Positioning sentence | A claim grounded in the customer's real alternative |
