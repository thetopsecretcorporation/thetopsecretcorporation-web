## Brief
Customers always have an alternative, even when that alternative is "do nothing." Map what they use today: manual work, spreadsheets, agencies, contractors, internal teams, incumbent platforms, direct competitors, delayed decisions, or tolerated pain.

An incumbent is the existing tool, process, vendor, internal habit, or political arrangement customers already rely on. It may be clunky, but it is familiar, budgeted, integrated, compliant enough, and socially safe.

April Dunford writes that positioning "defines how your product is a leader at delivering something that a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about." That leadership depends on the alternatives customers actually compare you against, not the competitors founders fear.

Use the ICP and JTBD evidence from the earlier guides as the filter. In business-to-business markets, switching often requires approval, budget, security, compliance, migration, and team retraining.

The status quo is often the real competitor. A better product does not automatically beat a trusted workflow.

## Exercise
Build the Status Quo & Switching Map from customer evidence.

### 01. Inventory real alternatives
Review discovery notes and highlight every mention of current behavior:

- Current tool, spreadsheet, manual process, or internal system.
- Agency, contractor, analyst, service provider, or internal team.
- Incumbent platform or direct competitor.
- Delayed decision, budget tradeoff, or "we will just keep doing this."

- Prior attempts, serious evaluations, or rejected options.
- Budget owner, approval path, or switching concern.

Only count an alternative as real if customers use it, pay for it, recently tried it, seriously evaluated it, or explicitly say they will keep doing it. A phantom competitor is a company the founder worries about but customers do not mention, use, compare against, or budget for.

### 02. Profile the top alternatives
For each important alternative, document:

- What the customer does today.
- Who performs the work and who owns the budget.
- What triggers the workflow.
- Tools, people, approvals, handoffs, and dependencies.

- What the alternative does well.
- What breaks, slows down, creates risk, or causes frustration.
- What the new product would have to preserve to be viable.

Do not only document weakness. The strengths of the current alternative become the minimum bar your product must clear.

### 03. Quantify status quo cost
Write the cost in concrete terms:

- Money: software spend, agency fees, labor cost, contractor cost, implementation cost.
- Time: hours per workflow, cycle time, waiting time, duplicated work.
- Risk: errors, compliance exposure, missed deadlines, data loss, security concerns.
- Opportunity cost: lost revenue, slower growth, lower conversion, fewer customers served.

- Emotional or social cost: stress, embarrassment, internal blame, executive pressure.

Strong statement:

> Today, revenue operations leaders solve territory planning by exporting customer relationship management data into spreadsheets, costing two analysts 10-15 hours per month and creating errors before quarterly planning.

Weak statement:

> Customers use spreadsheets because Salesforce is annoying.

### 04. Build the Four Forces map
Jobs-to-Be-Done Radio describes change through four forces: "The Push of the Current Situation," "The Pull of the New Solution," "The Anxiety of the New Solution," and "The Allegiance to the Current Situation." In plain language: pain, attraction, fear, and habit.

For the highest-priority alternative, map:

- Push: what makes the current way painful enough to motivate change.
- Pull: what better future attracts the customer to a new approach.
- Anxiety: what makes the new approach feel risky.
- Habit/allegiance: what workflow, contract, relationship, integration, political comfort, or familiar routine keeps the old way in place.

Switching happens when push and pull are strong enough to overcome anxiety and habit.

### 05. Write the switching-risk statement
Name why customers stay put even when the current process hurts.

Use this format:

> They do not switch because they fear [risk/anxiety] and are held in place by [habit/dependency].

Strong version:

> They do not switch because they fear Salesforce cleanup, executive approval, and team retraining.

Common risks include migration, setup work, security review, compliance review, budget approval, data quality, team adoption, reputation risk, vendor lock-in, and fear that the new product will not preserve what already works.

### 06. Define the minimum proof plan
Choose the lowest-friction proof that would make the next commitment feel safe:

- Workflow walkthrough or prototype.
- Manual concierge test or side-by-side benchmark.
- Return on investment calculation.
- Migration plan.

- Security or compliance documentation.
- Customer reference or case study.
- Free pilot, short paid pilot, or limited deployment.
- Done-for-you onboarding for one narrow workflow.

Early products usually win by finding a wedge where the current alternative is weakest, the customer has urgency, and the proof burden is small enough to overcome.

### 07. Turn the map into positioning
Use the evidence to complete this sentence:

> Compared with [real alternative], our product uniquely enables [capability], which creates [value] for [best-fit customer] in [high-urgency context].

If this sentence cannot be written with evidence, continue discovery before moving forward.

## Deliverable
Create a **Status Quo & Switching Map** and make it visible to the founding team and key employees. It should guide product scope, proof, positioning, pricing, and sales conversations.

Include:

- Real alternatives inventory.
- Phantom competitors separated from evidence-backed alternatives.
- Top status quo profile, including strengths and weaknesses.
- Prioritized enemy: the obstacle your product must beat first.

- Status quo cost statement.
- Four Forces map: push, pull, anxiety, and habit/allegiance.
- Switching-risk statement.
- Minimum proof plan.

- Positioning sentence based on a real alternative.

## Exit Criteria
Move on when:

- Real alternatives are based on customer behavior, not founder guesses.
- The top alternative includes strengths, not just weaknesses.
- Status quo cost is specific.

- Switching anxiety and habit are documented.
- The positioning sentence names the real alternative.
- Phantom competitors are separated from evidence-backed alternatives.
