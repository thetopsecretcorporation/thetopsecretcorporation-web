## Brief
A **wedge** is the narrow entry point where a specific customer has urgent pull for a specific problem and promise. After the first PMF scorecard, the job is not to add every requested feature. The job is to sharpen the wedge.

Eric Ries describes the startup loop this way: "The fundamental activity of a startup is to turn ideas into products, measure how customers respond, and then learn whether to pivot or persevere." A **pivot** changes a fundamental hypothesis. To **persevere** means continuing the current direction because evidence is improving.

Use Guide 09 evidence to decide what needs to change: ICP, problem, promise, product, channel, or business model. If the MVP created weak but useful signal, tighten the next test. If a high annual contract value (ACV) customer showed pull only because of bespoke work, separate real wedge evidence from custom services.

The operating principle is simple: **one change, one test, one decision.**

## Exercise
Start with the scorecard. Rahul Vohra's Superhuman process is useful because it segments the users who would be "very disappointed" without the product, then studies what makes that group different. Do the same with behavior, payment, and pull.

### 01. Read the PMF Scorecard.
Look for the segment with the strongest evidence: retained value events, repeat usage, paid conversion, renewal, expansion, referrals, faster sales cycles, high disappointment, or unusually specific customer language.

### 02. Study strongest-pull customers and near-converts.
Capture who they are, what triggered their need, what job or workflow they were trying to complete, what alternative they used before, what exact words they used, and what they did that proves they cared. Near-converts matter because their objections may reveal the next focused fix.

### 03. Diagnose the weak point.
Choose the most likely failure point:
- **Persona:** wrong customer, buyer, user, company size, urgency level, or use case.
- **Problem:** pain is too rare, mild, low-budget, or disconnected from a forcing event.
- **Promise:** value is real but explained in language the customer does not use or believe.
- **Product:** the product does not deliver the promised outcome reliably enough.

- **Channel:** acquisition is attracting low-urgency or wrong-fit users.
- **Business model:** users like it, but the buyer, package, or value metric does not support payment.

### 04. Choose one iteration path.
Narrow the ICP, focus the problem, reframe the promise, simplify the product, change the workflow, test price/packaging, change the acquisition channel, or return to discovery. Do not bundle five changes and call the result learning.

### 05. Decide iterate vs. pivot.
Ries asks the harder question: not "Can this product be built?" but "Should this product be built?" If evidence supports the same core wedge, iterate. If evidence invalidates a fundamental customer, problem, promise, product, channel, or business-model hypothesis, pivot. If the team cannot explain the evidence, revisit discovery.

### 06. Write the revised wedge hypothesis.
Use this structure:

> We believe [narrow ICP] has stronger pull because [specific urgent pain or trigger]. We will test this by [focused experiment] and continue only if [metric/threshold] happens by [date or sample size].

### 07. Design one pass/fail experiment.
Define the target segment, sample size, timebox, success metric, threshold, and decision rule before the test starts. Choose the metric closest to the risk: demand, activation, repeat use, paid conversion, renewal, expansion, referral, or sales-cycle speed.

### 08. Reject roadmap noise.
Do not add features because five indifferent users requested five unrelated things. April Dunford's positioning standard applies here: "Positioning defines how your product is a leader at delivering something that a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about."

## Deliverable
Produce a **Wedge Iteration Brief + Pass/Fail Experiment Card** that the whole founding team and key employees can see. This is the north-star artifact for the next validation cycle, not a private product note or loose roadmap.

April Dunford's own repositioning story is the right model: after most customers barely remembered the product, a small group showed transformed use. The lesson is not to average the market; it is to find where the product is already unusually valuable and position around that strength.

Your Wedge Iteration Brief must include:

- **Original wedge:** previous ICP, problem, promise, product focus, channel, and business-model assumption.
- **Evidence reviewed:** the Guide 09 PMF Scorecard signals that matter most.
- **Strongest-pull segment:** who retained, paid, expanded, referred, moved quickly, or said they would be very disappointed.
- **Diagnosis:** which hypothesis is weakest across persona, problem, promise, product, channel, or business model.

- **Revised wedge hypothesis:** the new narrow customer, urgent problem, promise, and reason to believe.
- **Focused experiment:** one test with target segment, sample size, timebox, metric, threshold, owner, and start/end dates.
- **Decision rule:** continue, iterate, pivot, revisit discovery, or stop.

Example:

> We believe founder-led business-to-business SaaS teams selling $20k+ annual contract value pilots have stronger pull because missed follow-up directly costs pipeline. We will test this with 25 founder-chief executive officers using a follow-up-prep workflow and continue only if 8 book demos, 3 start paid pilots, and 2 use it twice in the first week.

## Exit Criteria
Move forward only when this checklist is complete. Ries calls "validated learning" the unit of progress; the checklist should prove the next test will create a decision, not just activity.

- Wedge, pivot, and persevere are defined before use; PMF, ICP, MVP, and ACV follow the course-wide definitions.
- The revised wedge is based on Guide 09 evidence, not founder intuition or loud feedback.
- The strongest-pull segment is named.
- The weak point is diagnosed across persona, problem, promise, product, channel, or business model.

- Only one iteration path is chosen.
- The experiment has a target segment, sample size, timebox, success metric, threshold, owner, and decision rule.
- Roadmap additions are rejected unless they directly support the experiment.
- The Wedge Iteration Brief is visible to the founding team and key employees.
