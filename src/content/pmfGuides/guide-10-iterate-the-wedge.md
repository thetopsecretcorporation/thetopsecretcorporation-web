## Field Brief
Use what you learned from real customers to tighten the ICP, problem, promise, product, channel, or business model. Do not add random features. Find the narrow wedge where pull is strongest and make it sharper.

This is the Build-Measure-Learn loop in practice: put a focused version of the idea in front of customers, measure how they respond, and decide whether to persevere, iterate, pivot, or return to discovery.

## Why It Matters
Most startups do not hit PMF on the first try. Weak pull may mean the customer is wrong, the problem is not urgent enough, the promise is unclear, the product misses the workflow, the buyer will not pay, or the channel is attracting low-urgency users.

Iteration is not feature accumulation. A sharper wedge usually comes from narrowing, clarifying, or removing friction. The users with the strongest pull should teach you where the wedge is. Indifferent users should not drive the roadmap.

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this guide, founders should be able to:

| Skill | What it helps founders decide |
| --- | --- |
| Read the PMF scorecard | Where evidence is strongest and weakest. |
| Segment users by pull | Which customers deserve more attention than the average user. |
| Diagnose weak pull | Whether the issue is persona, problem, promise, product, or business model. |
| Choose one iteration path | What to test next, with a clear pass/fail experiment. |
| Produce the guide deliverable | A refined wedge statement and experiment that narrows who you serve, what you solve first, and why that entry point can expand. |

## Recommended Exercise
### 01. Start with the Guide 9 PMF scorecard
Review the evidence from the last cycle, then look for the signal that best explains customer pull.

| Scorecard signal | What to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Retention | Who keeps coming back without being pushed? |
| Repeat usage | Which workflow creates repeated behavior? |
| Paid conversion | Who will trade money for the outcome? |
| Renewal or expansion | Who wants more after the first cycle? |
| Referrals | Who is motivated enough to bring in others? |
| "Very disappointed" responses | Who would feel real loss if the product disappeared? |
| Sales-cycle speed or urgency | Who moves quickly because the pain is active now? |
| Strongest qualitative pull | Which customer language sounds urgent, specific, and repeated? |

Do not treat the scorecard as a grade. Treat it as a diagnostic tool. Ask where the evidence is strongest, where it is weak, and which part of the wedge needs to change.

### 02. Segment for strongest pull
Ignore averages first. Study the users who showed the most pull.

| Segment lens | Question to answer |
| --- | --- |
| Behavior | Which users came back, paid, renewed, expanded, or referred others? |
| Resilience | Which users pushed through bugs or rough edges? |
| Intensity | Which users said they would be most disappointed without it? |
| Language | Which users described the value in urgent, specific words? |

Then write a short segment readout:

| Readout field | Prompt |
| --- | --- |
| Strongest-pull customer | Who shows the strongest pull? |
| Trigger | What triggered their need? |
| Job or workflow | What were they trying to complete? |
| Previous alternative | What were they using before? |
| Customer language | What exact words did they use to describe the value? |
| Proof of care | What did they do, pay, repeat, or refer that proves they cared? |

After that, look at near-converts: users who wanted the same main benefit but did not fully adopt. Their objections may reveal a blocker worth removing. Indifferent users can be useful for pattern recognition, but they should not define the next roadmap.

### 03. Diagnose the weak point
Use a focused diagnosis to identify which hypothesis needs to change.

| Hypothesis | Diagnostic question |
| --- | --- |
| Persona | Are we targeting the wrong customer, buyer, user, company size, urgency level, or use case? |
| Problem | Is the pain too rare, mild, low-budget, or disconnected from a forcing event? |
| Promise | Is the value real but explained in language the customer does not use or believe? |
| Product | Does the product fail to deliver the promised outcome reliably enough? |
| Business model | If users like the product but will not pay, is the pain not budget-worthy, is the buyer wrong, or does the packaging fail to map to value? |

### 04. Choose one iteration path
Choose exactly one path for the next cycle. Do not run a broad bundle of changes.

| Path | Use it when evidence suggests... |
| --- | --- |
| Narrow the ICP | Retention, payment, urgency, or referral behavior is concentrated in one customer type. |
| Focus the problem | The same persona has a more painful adjacent problem. |
| Reframe the promise | Strongest-pull customers use sharper language than the team's current positioning. |
| Simplify the product | Repeat usage comes from one workflow more than the broader feature set. |
| Change the workflow | The current workflow blocks the promised outcome. |
| Test price or packaging | The team needs to learn whether the problem is budget-worthy. |
| Change the acquisition channel | The current channel reaches low-urgency users. |
| Revisit discovery | The team cannot explain why the evidence changed. |

Avoid bad iteration:

| Bad pattern | Why it weakens the wedge |
| --- | --- |
| Adding features because one user asked | It confuses request volume with evidence of pull. |
| Chasing every segment | It spreads learning across customers with different needs. |
| Averaging weak and strong feedback | It hides the segment that may actually retain. |
| Scaling acquisition to a leaky product | It burns through the market before the wedge works. |
| Treating experimentation as progress | Experiments only matter when they produce a decision. |

Do not scale acquisition until the wedge retains. More leads will not fix weak pull; they will only burn through the market faster.

### 05. Decide whether this is an iteration or a pivot
An iteration sharpens the same wedge. A pivot changes a fundamental hypothesis about who you serve, what problem you solve, what promise you make, how the product delivers, how the channel works, or how the business captures value.

| Decision | Use this when... |
| --- | --- |
| Persevere | The strongest segment shows increasing pull against the chosen metric. |
| Iterate | The segment is right, but the promise, product, channel, or packaging needs a focused adjustment. |
| Pivot | Evidence invalidates a fundamental persona, problem, promise, product, channel, or business-model hypothesis. |
| Revisit discovery | The team cannot explain what the evidence means or why behavior changed. |
| Stop or pause | Repeated focused tests fail, users show no urgency, buyers will not pay, and no identifiable segment shows stronger pull. |

### 06. Write the new wedge hypothesis
Use this structure:

> We believe [narrow ICP] has stronger pull because [specific urgent pain or trigger]. We will test this by [focused experiment] and continue only if [metric/threshold] happens by [date or sample size].

Example:

> We believe founder-led B2B SaaS teams selling $20k+ ACV pilots have stronger pull because missed follow-up directly costs pipeline. We will test this with 25 founder-CEOs using a follow-up-prep workflow and continue only if 8 book demos, 3 start paid pilots, and 2 use it twice in the first week.

### 07. Design one focused experiment
The experiment should test the riskiest part of the new wedge hypothesis.

| Experiment type | What it tests |
| --- | --- |
| New outreach list for the narrower ICP | Whether the refined customer segment responds. |
| Revised landing page or sales pitch using strongest-pull customer language | Whether the promise is clearer and more believable. |
| Narrower MVP workflow around the repeat-use moment | Whether the product can create more reliable value. |
| Different pricing or packaging test | Whether the problem is budget-worthy and mapped to value. |
| New onboarding flow for the activation bottleneck | Whether friction is preventing otherwise qualified users from reaching value. |
| Follow-up interviews with strongest-pull users and near-converts | Why the segment pulled harder and what still blocked adoption. |

Define the pass/fail criteria before the experiment starts.

| Criteria | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Target segment | The specific customer group being tested. |
| Sample size | How many prospects, users, buyers, or accounts are enough to make the test meaningful. |
| Time box | The deadline for reading the result. |
| Success metric | The behavior closest to the risk being tested. |
| Decision threshold | The number that determines continue, iterate, pivot, or stop. |

Pick the metric closest to the risk being tested:

| Risk | Useful metrics |
| --- | --- |
| Demand risk | Reply rate, booked calls, qualified pipeline, waitlist conversion, sales-cycle speed. |
| Value risk | Activation, repeat use, task completion, "very disappointed" percentage, qualitative intensity. |
| Revenue risk | Paid conversion, deposit, renewal, expansion, willingness-to-pay signal. |
| Referral risk | Intros, organic sharing, team invites, customer-sourced leads. |

## Guide Deliverable
Produce a structured wedge iteration deliverable with:

| Deliverable field | What to include |
| --- | --- |
| Original wedge | The previous ICP, problem, promise, and product focus. |
| Evidence reviewed | The Guide 9 PMF scorecard signals and strongest qualitative evidence. |
| Strongest-pull segment | Who showed the most retention, payment, urgency, referrals, or "very disappointed" responses. |
| 4 Ps diagnosis | What changed or remains uncertain across persona, problem, promise, and product. |
| Business-model note | Whether willingness to pay, buyer identity, or packaging needs validation. |
| New wedge hypothesis | The revised ICP/problem/promise/product statement. |
| Focused experiment | One test with sample size, time box, metric, and threshold. |
| Decision rule | Continue, iterate, pivot, revisit discovery, or stop. |

Example output:

| Deliverable field | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Original wedge | AI assistant for sales teams. |
| Evidence | Only founder-led B2B SaaS teams used it weekly and said they would be very disappointed without automated follow-up prep. |
| Diagnosis | Persona was too broad; the problem was strongest when missed follow-up directly threatened pipeline. |
| New wedge | Founder-led B2B SaaS teams selling $20k+ ACV pilots need follow-up prep after sales calls. |
| Experiment | Contact 25 founder-CEOs with a follow-up-prep workflow. |
| Success threshold | Continue only if 8 book demos, 3 start paid pilots, and 2 use it twice in the first week. |
| Decision | Persevere, iterate, pivot, revisit discovery, or stop based on the threshold. |
