## Brief
Do not use growth to prove the product works. First prove that one beachhead segment repeatedly receives value, keeps using or paying, and pulls more from the company.

Sam Altman states the sequence plainly: "Then they need to build a product some users really love. Only after that they should focus on growth above all else." His warning is just as important: "A startup that prematurely targets a growth goal often ends up making a nebulous product that some users sort of like and papering over this with 'growth hacking'."

Use the course definitions for PMF and ICP. For scale decisions, define only the terms that change the decision: **Rule of 40**, the software-as-a-service heuristic where growth rate plus profit margin totals 40% or more; **beachhead**, the first narrow market segment to win before expanding; **payback**, the time required to recover customer acquisition cost; and **adjacent segment**, the next customer segment close enough that current proof transfers.

Scale is a gate, not a brainstorm. Growth levers should amplify what already works, not hide weak PMF.

## Exercise
Brian Balfour gives the core rule: "Without retention, accelerating growth is meaningless. Your retention curve is the best proof." Work through this as a readiness assessment before choosing a growth lever.

### 01. Define retention for this product.
State the value event, natural usage frequency, expected value window, and beachhead cohort to inspect. A daily collaboration product, monthly business-to-business workflow, and seasonal product need different retention windows.

### 02. Confirm cohort retention and meaningful usage.
Check whether retained customers keep performing the value event, not just logging in. Segment by beachhead, source, use case, activation path, and customer type. Aggregate retention can hide the fact that no specific segment is actually working.

### 03. Confirm pull.
Look for referrals, expansion, inbound requests, faster sales cycles, tolerance of rough edges, customer-initiated introductions, or demand that creates capacity pressure. Marc Andreessen describes strong markets as places where "the market pulls product out of the startup."

### 04. Confirm repeatable sales or acquisition.
The company should be able to document the ICP, qualification rules, channel, pitch, objection handling, pricing, close path, onboarding, and reference customers. If the founder cannot explain why any deal closes, hiring sales will spread confusion.

### 05. Confirm delivery capacity.
Review onboarding, implementation, support, reliability, customer success, and time to first value. If growth will damage the experience, the bottleneck is delivery, not demand.

### 06. Identify one bottleneck.
Choose the main constraint: demand creation, activation, onboarding, retention, pricing, expansion, reliability, support capacity, implementation burden, or sales cycle. If everything is the bottleneck, the company is not ready to scale.

### 07. Pick one scaling lever.
Choose one: founder-led distribution, referrals, content/community, paid acquisition, partnerships, sales hire, customer success system, product onboarding, activation flow, or reliability improvement. Explain why this lever now and why the others are not first.

### 08. Design one lever test.
Define the hypothesis, input metric, quality metric, stop condition, owner, and timebox. The quality metric must stay close to retained value, activated accounts, qualified pipeline, renewal, expansion, or payback.

### 09. Make the stage decision.
Choose scale, transition, keep iterating, or refocus beachhead. Michael Seibel's product-market fit standard is intentionally demanding: you have reached it when you are "overwhelmed with usage." Most teams should scale one lever only after the evidence is specific, retained, and repeatable.

## Deliverable
Produce a **Scale Readiness Assessment + One Lever Test Card** that the whole founding team and key employees can see. This is the north-star artifact for deciding whether to add channels, hires, spend, systems, or adjacent markets.

First Round's Levels of PMF frames the bar well: "Repeatability is the holy grail on the hunt for product-market fit." The assessment must prove the company is not mistaking acquisition for fit.

Your Scale Readiness Assessment must include:

- **Retention evidence:** retention definition, natural usage frequency, beachhead cohort view, meaningful usage, payment/renewal/expansion, and churn reasons.
- **Pull evidence:** referrals, inbound demand, expansion requests, faster response, shortened sales cycles, customer introductions, or tolerance of rough edges.
- **Repeatability evidence:** documented ICP, qualification, pitch, acquisition/sales channel, onboarding, success process, pricing, objection handling, and reference customers.
- **Capacity evidence:** onboarding load, support burden, reliability, implementation requirements, customer success coverage, and time to first value.

- **False scale signals:** the growth metrics the team will not overvalue this cycle.
- **Scaling decision:** scale, transition, keep iterating, or refocus beachhead.
- **One lever test:** bottleneck, lever, hypothesis, input metric, quality metric, owner, timebox, and stop condition.

Use these decision definitions:
- **Scale** when retention, pull, repeatability, and capacity are evidenced in the beachhead; choose one lever.
- **Transition** when retention works in a narrow segment but the sales/channel/system still needs documentation.
- **Keep iterating** when retention or pull is weak; return to activation, onboarding, value proposition, or discovery.
- **Refocus beachhead** when one segment retains materially better than the rest; narrow the ICP and stop serving weak-fit segments.

For adjacent expansion, Geoffrey Moore's discipline applies: avoid "chasing one-off opportunities elsewhere." The next segment should be big enough to matter, small enough to lead, and close enough that current proof transfers.

## Exit Criteria
Move forward only when this checklist is complete. Altman's "Before Growth" standard is the filter: growth comes after some users really love the product.

- Rule of 40, beachhead, payback, and adjacent segment are defined before use; PMF and ICP follow the course-wide definitions.
- Retention definition matches the product's natural usage frequency.
- Cohort retention is segmented by beachhead, not just aggregate.
- Meaningful usage is tied to the value event.

- Churn reasons are known and segmented.
- Pull evidence exists: referrals, expansion, inbound, faster sales, customer introductions, or rough-edge tolerance.
- Sales or acquisition motion is documented enough to teach.
- Onboarding and support can handle the next increment of demand.

- One bottleneck is selected.
- One scaling lever has a hypothesis, input metric, quality metric, stop condition, owner, and timebox.
- The decision is scale, transition, keep iterating, or refocus beachhead.
- The Scale Readiness Assessment is visible to the founding team and key employees.
